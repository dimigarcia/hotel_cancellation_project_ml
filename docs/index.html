Predicting hotel booking cancellations

A model that scores every new booking at two Portuguese hotels for its risk of being cancelled, so Revenue Management can act before the room goes empty.

The business problem

Two errors, two costs

A cancellation the hotel doesn't see coming leaves a room empty at cost price. Acting on a guest who was never going to cancel (asking for a deposit, for instance) can annoy a loyal customer. The model has to balance both, and it may only use what is known at the moment of booking.

Data leakage

Five variables left out

These columns are only known after the outcome, or reveal it directly. Using them would make the model look excellent in testing and fail in real use.

reservation_status

It is the outcome itself: "Canceled" maps one-to-one to the target.

reservation_status_date

The date the outcome was recorded.

assigned_room_type

Assigned at check-in, which cancelled bookings never reach.

booking_changes

Counted until check-in or cancellation, so only final after the outcome.

required_car_parking_spaces

Zero cancellations among 7,416 bookings with parking. That suggests it is filled in on arrival, so it is excluded as a precaution.

Method

Train on the past, test on the future

The data is split by arrival date, as it would be in production. The test period is used once, at the end.

TrainJul 2015 – Dec 201678,589 rows · 36.2% cancelled
ValidationJan – Apr 201718,442 · 36.5%
TestMay – Aug 201722,177 · 40.6%
  • Feature engineering: eight new variables, each with a business reason. Examples are total guests, total nights, travelling with children, the guest's past cancellation ratio, price per person and weekend-only stays.
  • Models: a logistic regression baseline, a random forest, and LightGBM. LightGBM was then tuned with Optuna (40 trials, scored on the validation period).
  • Imbalance: class weights make a missed cancellation count more during training. The decision threshold (0.36) was then chosen on validation to maximise F1, balancing the two errors.

What drives the score (SHAP)

↑Non-refundable deposit: the strongest single signal, mostly group or agency blocks that get released.
↑Portuguese origin raises risk; foreign guests lower it.
↓Any special request clearly lowers risk: the guest intends to come.
↑Long lead time raises risk; last-minute bookings are safest.
↑Online travel agents and transient customers raise risk; contracts lower it.
↑Higher price and a history of cancelling push risk up.
Conclusions

Highest-risk profile

A booking made months in advance, through groups or travel agents, from Portugal, at the City Hotel, with no special requests. The extreme case is a non-refundable group block, which is released almost every time. The lowest risk comes from returning, direct or corporate guests booking close to arrival who have asked for something specific.

Proposed use

  • Score every booking when it is made and give Revenue Management a daily list of bookings with a risk of 0.36 or more.
  • Flagged individual bookings: send a reconfirmation email about 30 days before arrival, or offer a discounted upgrade in exchange for non-refundable terms.
  • Group and agency blocks: set release deadlines and ask for partial deposits.
  • Overbooking: the sum of predicted probabilities for a night is the expected number of cancellations. Use it to set a per-night level, tighter at the City Hotel and in the Resort Hotel's summer.
  • Protect loyal guests: never apply restrictive measures to returning guests or guests with special requests.

Next steps

  • Put a euro value on each error with Revenue Management and choose a cost-minimising threshold.
  • Confirm when country and parking are recorded, to rule out remaining leakage.
  • Retrain quarterly, and A/B test the preventive actions to measure the cancellations they avoid.
